Plate tectonics is the movement of large sections of Earth's crust, which drives the formation of mountain ranges through various geological processes. When tectonic plates collide, one may be forced beneath the other in a process called subduction, or they may slide past each other. The immense pressure and heat generated can cause the crust to buckle and rise, forming towering peaks.

Here are three examples of mountain ranges shaped by plate tectonics:

1. **The Himalayas** – Formed by the collision of the Indian Plate and the Eurasian Plate, the Himalayas are the tallest mountain range on Earth. As the Indian Plate pushes northward, it crumples against the Eurasian Plate, uplifting the land over millions of years. The ongoing movement continues to push the mountains upward, though erosion plays a role in shaping their contours.

2. **The Andes** – Located along the western edge of South America, the Andes were created by the subduction of the Nazca Plate beneath the South American Plate. The intense pressure and volcanic activity have led to the formation of a long chain of mountains, including some of the highest peaks in the Western Hemisphere. The movement of these plates also contributes to frequent earthquakes in the region.

3. **The Rockies** – Stretching across western North America, the Rockies emerged from the compression of the North American Plate and the interaction with the Cocos Plate (though primarily driven by tectonic uplift in the region). As tectonic forces pushed the land upward, particularly during periods of crustal thickening, the Rockies were formed. The range includes dramatic peaks like Mount Elbert and Mount Rushmore.

In each case, the dynamic forces of plate movement—whether through collision, subduction, or shear—have been instrumental in creating vast, enduring mountain systems. The timing and intensity of these events vary, leading to unique geological features. Over time, weathering and erosion modify the shapes of these ranges, but the foundational structure is often preserved due to the strength of the crustal uplift.

These examples illustrate how plate tectonics not only creates new land but also influences climate, water flow, and biodiversity in the regions they affect. The continuous motion of Earth’s lithosphere ensures that mountain ranges are a testament to the planet’s ever-changing geology.
